Anatomy of a Point

One real LiDAR sweep, pulled apart to the last byte. Every number on this page was measured off the actual file — nothing here is simulated or illustrative.

The same instant, two sensors

This is the scene

Keep this image in your head. Below, the same scene arrives as 121,015 rows of four numbers. Everything a network has to figure out — that there is a road, parked cars on the right, buildings, a cyclist — has to come out of those numbers alone. No colour. No texture. No pixels.
The file

No header, no field names, no point count

A KITTI Velodyne scan is a .bin file, and it is one of the least helpful file formats in computer vision: a flat wall of little-endian float32 with nothing to tell you what any of it means. You are simply expected to know.


    

      
Four floats per point: x, y, z, intensity. And the frame those coordinates live in — x forward, y left, z up, origin at the laser diode 1.73 m above the road — is nowhere in the file either. Get the convention wrong and your entire scene is silently rotated, with no error anywhere.

    What the sensor actually measured
    
Cartesian is a lie the file tells you

    

      
Click a point above and look at the two halves of its record. The left half — x, y, z — is what's stored. The right half — range, azimuth, elevation — is what the sensor measured.

      
A spinning LiDAR does not know where anything is in Cartesian space. It knows three things: how long the pulse took to come back (range), which way the head was pointing when it fired (azimuth), and which of the 64 stacked lasers fired (elevation). The x, y, z in the file is a convenience conversion applied afterwards:

    

    
x = r · cos(elev) · cos(azim)
y = r · cos(elev) · sin(azim)
z = r · sin(elev)

    

      This is not trivia — it is the seed of your whole architecture. The sensor's native coordinate system is spherical. Its samples are uniform in angle, not in space. Every consequence you will meet later — density collapsing with range, range images working at all, cylindrical voxels beating cubic ones — comes straight back to these three lines.
    

  






  

    The hidden structure
    
It isn't a blob. It's 64 nested cones.

    

      
Set the colouring to ring and drag the ring slider. Each laser sits at a fixed elevation angle and paints one closed loop on the world. KITTI throws the laser id away, but it's recoverable — within one laser the azimuth climbs steadily and then wraps, so the wraps are the ring boundaries. There are exactly 64 of them.

      
Now unroll those cones. Put ring number on the vertical axis and azimuth on the horizontal, and the unordered set becomes a dense, ordinary image:

      Look what just happened. An unordered set of 121,015 points with no neighbour structure became a grid — and a grid is something an ordinary 2D CNN can process at 100 FPS. That single move is the entire idea behind RangeNet++, SalsaNext and CENet, which you'll meet in Phase 7.
      


      And look at the cost, which is equally visible: the image is full of black gaps where nothing returned, distant objects are squeezed into a handful of pixels, and any two surfaces along the same ray collapsed into one — whatever was behind is simply gone.

    Your question, directly
    
How you reconstruct a scene from points

    

      
"Reconstruction" means six different things, and they form a ladder. Each rung buys structure the rung below didn't have — and the top of the ladder is where your problem statement lives.

    

    

      
L0

        
Render the points

        
What the explorer above does. The cloud already is the scene, sampled. Your eye does the reconstruction. Good enough to debug with, useless to a planner.

      

      
L1

        
Re-impose order — the range image

        
Invert the spherical projection to recover the (ring, azimuth) grid. Free, lossless-ish for a single sweep, and instantly makes 2D convolutions and neighbour lookups legal.

      

      
L2

        
Discretise space — voxels or occupancy

        
Bin points into cells and keep an occupancy or feature vector per cell. Fixes the "unordered set" problem for good. Pay for it in the emptiness you just measured — hence sparse tensors.

      

      
L3

        
Fit surfaces

        
Estimate a normal per point from its neighbours, then run Poisson reconstruction, ball pivoting, or a TSDF to get a watertight mesh. This is what people usually mean by "3D reconstruction". Beautiful, slow, and largely irrelevant to driving.

      

      
L4

        
Accumulate over time

        
Register consecutive sweeps with ICP or a SLAM system, transform them into a common frame, and stack them. This is the only rung that recovers occluded geometry — because you drive past and look again. It is also where moving objects start smearing into streaks, which is the problem Phase 7's motion models exist to solve.

      

      
L5

        
Label it, then flatten it

        
Classify every point, project down into a 2.5D grid carrying height plus semantics. This is PS 26053. Notice it is not L3 — you are not building a mesh, you are building a labelled heightfield, and that is a far cheaper target.

      

    

    

      The one hard limit. No rung invents data. Everything hidden behind that parked car in the image is absent from the file, and no reconstruction recovers it — only motion (L4) or a prior does. Reconstruction reorganises what was measured. It never adds.
