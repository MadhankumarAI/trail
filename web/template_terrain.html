The controls. 2.5D / 3D — the same height field drawn two ways: straight down, or projected and orbitable (drag it). 2.5D is the cheaper one. height × — vertical exaggeration. The ground here has about 1 m of relief across 80 m, so at ×1 it is a plane; raise it to see camber and kerbs. It scales the picture only, never a number. objects — the detector's output: static obstacles as pale columns shaded by how far they stand above the ground, and Car / Pedestrian / Cyclist as outlined boxes with a spur showing which way they face. relief — hillshading. Each cell is lit according to the slope of the height field around it, so ridges, kerbs and the road crown pick up light and shade. Colour still says what the cell is; the shading says what shape it is. Turn it off and the map goes back to flat colour. shade by height — replaces the drivability colours with an elevation ramp, low to high, with the scale in metres beside the legend. range rings — 10, 20, 30 and 50 m circles about the vehicle.

Top-down, vehicle at centre facing up, m half-width. Cells are m — a coarsening of the map's m lattice, since a 5 cm cell holds one or two returns and carries no surface statistic. A cell is drawn only once it has enough ground returns to be judged; everything else is absent, which is why the left panel thins out with range while the right one does not.

Why the panels differ. Accumulation is not free. Merged naively it is worse than a single frame, because the same patch of road disagrees between sweeps by a median 7.1 cm — systematically, ramping about 24 cm over 20 m of travel, which is a ~0.7° pitch error in the pose chain. That lands as apparent roughness and condemns real road. Each incoming sweep is therefore aligned in z against the map it overlaps, and only returns inside the m trust gate are ingested at all: beyond that an observation is biased rather than merely noisy, and averaging a biased estimate with a good one stays biased.

What this does not do. Because only returns inside the trust gate are ingested, the accumulated map reaches much further behind the vehicle than in front of it — at the last frame it spans m along the direction of travel. The gain is dense, reliable memory of ground the vehicle has driven past and is still manoeuvring in, and terrain that was distant earlier being resolved properly once approached. It is not extra forward reach: nothing here tells you about road 50 m ahead that has not been observed from close range yet. The ahead and behind rows are reported separately for that reason.

2.5D, and why that is the cheap option. An elevation map stores one height per cell of a 2D lattice — a height field, not a volume. That is the representation here: ix, iy on the lattice, a height, its variance, and a drivability class: seven bytes a cell. The accumulated ground layer above is 7,801 cells, 53 KiB. A dense 3D voxel grid over the same footprint would be 360,000 to 1,400,000 cells depending on z-step — 46 to 179 times as many — to hold information the ground does not have, because a ground surface is exactly one height per column. Planning reduces to two dimensions while slopes, kerbs and steps stay measurable. No stage of this pipeline bins in z.

On the elevation. Switch to 3D and the surface looks nearly flat at ×1, because it is: measured on this map the ground spans 105 cm of relief across an 80 m scene, a vertical-to-horizontal ratio of about 1:76. A road drawn to scale is a plane. The height is real all the same, and the cross-road profile shows it — road centre at −1.96 m, falling 0.6° to the far side, with the kerb and pavement standing 35 cm proud at y = −6 to −10 m. That 35 cm step is precisely what the step term rejects as non-drivable, so the red band along the kerb in the top-down view and the ledge in the 3D view are the same measurement seen twice. Exaggerate the vertical to see it; every number in the table still comes from the true heights.

2.5D shows this without a camera. The default view shades each cell by the gradient of the height field, so relief reads directly on the flat map — one pass over the cells, no projection and no depth sort. Measured over these 24 frames, both panels: flat fill 1.1 ms, relief 4.6 ms, 3D projection 10.5 ms per frame. The 3D toggle is kept because a ledge is easier to believe when you can orbit it, but it draws the same height field rather than a different representation, and it is the more expensive way.

A viewer showing tall buildings is rendering a different layer: the top surface of every occupied cell. This one renders only the ground, because drivability is a property of the ground. Neither is more correct — they answer different questions, and the same 2.5D cells carry both.
