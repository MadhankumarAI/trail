Five Ways to Find a Neighbour

Every architecture for point clouds is answering one question, and it is not the question the paper titles suggest. Each demo below runs the actual mechanism on real points from your KITTI scan.

The spine

Convolution needs neighbours. A set has none.

In Phase 2 we shuffled all 121,015 rows of the scan and every measurable property came out identical. The row number carries no information. But convolution, attention, and any notion of "local shape" all need to know which points are near which.

So every family below invents a neighbourhood definition. That single choice determines the architecture, its speed, and its failure modes. Everything else is detail.

Why max-pooling, of all things

A network that reads a set must return the same answer for every ordering of that set. There are 121,015! orderings of your scan. The only way to survive that is a symmetric function — one whose output genuinely does not depend on input order.

That is the whole of PointNet's contribution. Push every point through the same little MLP, then collapse with max. Order-proof by construction, and it handles any number of points because max doesn't care how many you give it. Both of Phase 2's first two constraints, solved in one line.

Sum works too, and some networks use it. Max wins in practice because it is robust to the density variation we measured — a cell with 200 points and a cell with 6 give comparable maxima, but wildly different sums.
Family 1a · PointNet

The network only ever sees a skeleton

Max-pooling has a strange consequence. For each output feature, exactly one point supplies the maximum. Every other point contributes nothing to that dimension. The points that win at least one dimension are called the critical point set — and they are the only points the global feature actually depends on.

Below is a real object from your scan: 787 points, , sitting from the sensor. Vehicle-sized. Drag to rotate. The slider sets how many features the layer computes.

feature dims K 64
Slide K down to 16 and watch. A handful of points — always the extremities, the silhouette — carry the entire global feature. Everything interior is invisible to the network. Push K up and the set grows, but it saturates: past a few hundred features you are re-selecting the same outline.

This is why plain PointNet cannot segment a street. It knows the convex outline of the whole scene and nothing about what is happening at any particular point. The fix has to be locality.
Family 1b · PointNet++

Set abstraction, step by step

PointNet++ builds locality by hand, in three moves that repeat at every level. Step through them on the same object.

Sample — pick M centroids with farthest-point sampling, so they spread out instead of clumping where the points are dense. Group — collect every point within radius r of each centroid. Pool — run a mini-PointNet on each group, so each ball becomes one feature vector at its centroid. The cloud is now M points with richer features, and you do it again with a bigger radius.

M32
radius0.45 m
Now look at the cost readout. Farthest-point sampling is inherently sequential — each centroid needs the distance from every point to every centroid already chosen. On this 787-point object that is trivial. On the full 121,015-point scan, with the M values real networks use, it is the reason PointNet++ runs at 1–2 FPS and scores around 20 mIoU on KITTI-scale data.

RandLA-Net's entire insight is to delete this step. Replace farthest-point sampling with random sampling — which is free — and compensate for the points you carelessly drop with a local feature aggregation module that widens the receptive field fast. That one substitution takes the family from unusable to ~54 mIoU at real speed.
Family 2 · projection

Or: cheat, and make it an image again

You already built this one in Phase 2. Bin by (ring, azimuth) and the unordered set becomes a dense 2D array where neighbours are simply adjacent pixels. Ordinary 2D CNNs apply, and they run at 30–100 FPS.

RangeNet++ (52 mIoU) → SalsaNext (~59) → CENet (~65). The lineage is mostly better 2D backbones and better loss functions on the same projection.

The cost was visible in your own range image: black gaps everywhere, distant objects squeezed into a few pixels, and any two surfaces along one ray collapsed into one — whatever was behind is gone, not hidden. Thin structures land on pixel boundaries and blur away, which hurts poles and pedestrians specifically. RangeNet++ needs a GPU nearest-neighbour post-pass just to undo its own projection damage.

Family 3 · sparse voxels

Bin it, then only compute where something is

Drop points into a grid and the cell index becomes the ordering — permutation invariance for free, and neighbours are grid-adjacent cells so 3D convolution applies directly.

Phase 2 measured the catch: at 5 cm the grid is 99.9955% empty. So store only occupied cells in a hash table and compute only there. Empty space costs nothing. That is sparse convolution, and that number is why it exists.

Below is a top-down view of the non-ground points within 24 m of your sensor. We're looking straight down purely so the grid is visible; the real thing is 3D.

conv layers0
Drag "conv layers" up with regular sparse selected. A regular sparse convolution writes an output wherever its kernel touches an active site — so the active set dilates outward one ring per layer. After a few layers your beautifully sparse tensor has gone dense and the entire advantage is gone.

Submanifold sparse convolution fixes it by refusing to create new active sites: output exists only where input existed. Compare the two counts. This distinction is the single most important implementation detail in the whole family, and it is the thing people miss when their sparse network mysteriously runs out of VRAM at layer six.
MinkowskiNet / MinkUNet is the canonical implementation, ~63 mIoU at 13–36 FPS. SPVCNN notes that voxelising smears small objects — a distant pedestrian becomes three cells with no shape — so it runs a point branch alongside the voxel branch and fuses them, recovering exactly the pedestrian and pole classes you are graded on.
Family 4 · cylindrical

Bin the way the sensor measures

Phase 2 measured density collapsing from 40.9 points/m³ in the near field to 0.007 at 40–80 m — a factor of about 5,000. A uniform Cartesian grid ignores that completely: every cell is the same size, so near cells overflow and far cells sit empty.

Cylinder3D asks the obvious question. The sensor measures in (range, azimuth, elevation) — so why bin in (x, y, z)? Bin in (ρ, θ, z) instead and cells grow with range automatically, at roughly the rate density falls.

Switch the grid and watch the colouring even out. Cell colour is points-per-cell, on the same scale for both.

resolutionmedium

Measured on all 104,039 points inside 24 m

Read the p95 column. That is the spread the network actually has to cope with. Cartesian cells are far more unequal — a handful hold hundreds of points while most hold a few. Cylindrical cells are much more evenly loaded, which means every cell's pooled feature is computed from a comparable sample. Better-conditioned inputs, and an inductive bias that matches the physics we measured rather than fighting it.

Cylinder3D adds two more pieces on top: asymmetrical residual blocks, with kernels shaped like cars and poles instead of cubes, and a dimension-decomposition context module that builds high-rank global context cheaply from per-axis low-rank parts. Together: ~68 mIoU at ~10 FPS — the best accuracy-per-frame in the practical range.
Family 5 · serialised attention

The ceiling, and why you probably won't ship it

Point Transformer V3 (2024) sidesteps neighbour search entirely. It orders points along a space-filling curve — Hilbert or Z-order — which has the property that points close along the curve are usually close in space. Then it runs attention over contiguous patches of that sequence. No kNN, no ball query, no voxel hashing: just sort, then attend.

It trades exact neighbourhoods for scale, and it wins — low-to-mid 70s mIoU, the current leader on large outdoor benchmarks. SphereFormer takes a related route with radial window attention, specifically to help sparse distant points.

Why it is not your answer: heavy to train, hungry for VRAM, and every point of mIoU costs frames — while your problem statement grades latency explicitly. On a 4 GB GTX 1650 this is not a realistic training target. Cite it as the ceiling and as evidence you know where the field is.
