Phase 1 · Foundations

The Half Dimension

What "2.5D" actually means, why it sits between a picture and a volume, and exactly which things it can no longer tell you. Drive the simulator below — everything here is something you can see happen.

The mental model

It's about what is a function of what

Forget "dimensions" for a second. Every representation here is a lookup: you hand it a coordinate, it hands you back a value. The whole distinction between 2D, 2.5D and 3D is what you're allowed to ask for, and how many answers come back.

Watch what happens to the answer count as you move across these four:

I(u, v) → colour

2D image

Ask for a pixel, get a colour. No geometry at all — a photo of a wall and a photo of a photo of a wall are identical data.

one answer · no depth
D(u, v) → depth

2.5D · depth map

Ask for a pixel, get how far away that pixel is. Geometry arrives — but only the first surface each ray touches.

one answer · per ray
H(x, y) → height

2.5D · elevation map

Ask for a spot on the ground, get the height of the surface there. A heightfield, like terrain in a video game.

one answer · per column
O(x, y, z) → occupied?

3D

Ask about any point in space, get whether matter is there. Now a single ground position can have many answers stacked above it.

many answers · per column
That is the entire idea. 2.5D is 3D data forced to be a single-valued function of a 2D domain. The "0.5" is the one extra number you're allowed to store per cell. Everything 2.5D can't do follows mechanically from that constraint: any situation needing two surfaces along the same axis — a branch above a road, a bridge over a river, a tunnel, a table with floor beneath it — cannot be written down.

What you should have noticed

Four things the simulator just proved

1 · Angular spacing means density collapses with range. Drop the beam count to 16 and watch the far half of the street nearly vanish while the ground in front of the bumper stays solid. Beams are uniform in angle, not in distance — so at 3 m adjacent returns land centimetres apart, and at 35 m they land most of a metre apart. Nothing in this picture is a modelling choice; it's trigonometry. Remember this, because in a later phase it becomes the entire justification for the adaptive grid.

2 · No return does not mean empty. In mode 3 the region behind the car is grey, not white. The sensor learned nothing there. A 2D occupancy grid that only has free and occupied is lying to you — it needs a third state, unknown, and treating unknown as free is how robots drive into things.

3 · 2D occupancy destroys exactly the hazards you care about. The pothole reads as free floor. The kerb reads as free floor. And the overhanging branch — which you could comfortably drive under — reads as a solid wall across the road. All three failures come from the same cause: the height axis was thrown away, so "there is matter in this column" was all that survived.

4 · 2.5D fixes two of those three, and can't fix the third. Modes 4 and 5 both recover the pothole and the kerb — real height, correctly signed. But look at what happens at the branch. z_max reports 3.8 m and the branch reads as a wall again. z_min reports 0 m and the branch disappears entirely, so a tall vehicle drives straight into it. There is no third option, because a single number per column cannot hold two surfaces. This is not an implementation flaw you can engineer around — it's the definition of 2.5D.

The last row is the honest one. Occlusion is not a weakness of 2.5D — full 3D can't recover it either. A single lidar from a single viewpoint fundamentally cannot see through a car. That's why perception systems accumulate over time and motion, not just over space. Keep that separate in your head from the 2.5D limitation, because they get conflated constantly.
A distinction almost everyone blurs

There are two different 2.5Ds

Both are called 2.5D, both store one number per cell, and they are not the same thing. They differ in which axis got collapsed.

view-centric

Depth map / range image

Domain is the sensor's own view — pixel row and column, or beam and azimuth. Single-valued along each ray. Collapses everything behind the first surface.

loses: occluded geometry
world-centric

Elevation map / heightfield

Domain is the ground plane — a fixed x, y grid in the world. Single-valued along gravity. Collapses everything stacked vertically.

loses: overhangs, bridges, tunnels
Why you specifically need to keep these apart. Your problem statement asks for a world-centric 2.5D map as the output. But several of the fastest models you'll meet later work by converting the point cloud into a view-centric range image first. So a real pipeline can be 2.5D twice over, in two different senses, with a full 3D point cloud in between. If you only have one bucket labelled "2.5D" in your head, that architecture will be incomprehensible.
Before we move on

Check yourself

Don't look anything up. If a question feels shaky, say so and we'll stay on Phase 1.

  1. A drone maps a multi-storey car park. Is a 2.5D elevation map enough? Why or why not — and what specifically breaks?

  2. Your elevation map stores z_max per cell. A cell reads 2.4 m. Name three physically different scenes that produce that identical reading.

  3. Is a stereo camera's disparity map 2D, 2.5D, or 3D? Which of the two flavours above?

  4. Why does a uniform 5 cm grid start wasting memory at long range — and what does that have to do with beams being spaced by angle?

  5. You store both z_min and z_max per cell. Have you now got 3D? What can you represent that you couldn't before, and what still can't you represent?
